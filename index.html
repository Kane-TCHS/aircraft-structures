The Four Forces of Flight

Every aircraft in flight is pushed and pulled by four forces at once.

Background

  • Weight — the gravitational attraction of the Earth. Always pulls straight down.
  • Lift — created by the effect of airflow as it passes over and under the wing. Acts upward.
  • Thrust — the forward-acting force, produced by the power plant, which opposes drag.
  • Drag — resistance from the air; acts in the opposite direction of flight.

In steady, level flight these balance out: lift equals weight, and thrust equals drag.

02

The Five Main Structures

Nearly every airplane is built from the same five major structures.

Background

  • Fuselage — the principal structural unit. It holds the crew, passengers, and cargo, and everything else attaches to it.
  • Wing — the airfoils on each side of the fuselage. They are the main lifting surfaces that support the airplane in flight.
  • Empennage — the tail section (vertical stabilizer, horizontal stabilizer, rudder, elevator). It keeps the aircraft stable and pointed into the wind, like the feathers on an arrow.
  • Landing Gear — absorbs the energy of landing and supports the aircraft on the ground. May be fixed, or retractable for less drag.
  • Power Plant — produces thrust. On a small aircraft it is an engine turning a propeller.

03

Power Plants

The power plant produces thrust, but it takes very different forms depending on how fast the aircraft flies.

Background

  • Piston engine + propeller — like a car engine, used on most small aircraft.
  • Rocket engine — carries its own fuel and oxidizer; used on the Bell X-1 to break the sound barrier.
  • Turbofan — a gas turbine spinning a large fan; produces thrust by expelling a jet of hot gases. Standard on commercial airliners.
  • High-speed jet engine — on very fast fighters, the engine has few moving parts and relies on the aircraft's forward motion to compress the incoming air.

Power output climbs enormously with era and mission: a 1903 engine, a modern light-plane engine, and an airliner's engines are worlds apart.

Fuselage & Mission

A fuselage is sized for its job: how many people or how much cargo it carries, and how far it flies.

Background

The fuselage is the body everything attaches to, so its length tracks the mission. A single-seat or two-seat light aircraft needs only a short body. A fighter is bigger, but still built around one or two crew. An airliner has to seat many rows of passengers, so its fuselage is far longer.

Wing Placement & Configuration

Where the wing sits, and how many wings there are, changes how the aircraft performs.

Background — where the wing sits

The wing can attach high, in the middle, or low on the fuselage.

  • High wing — sits on top of the fuselage, so the body doesn't block the air flowing under the wing. Airflow stays continuous with minimal interruption, so a high wing generates the most lift. It also gives the pilot a clear downward view.
  • Mid wing — passes through the middle of the fuselage, which interrupts the airflow the most, so it generates the least lift.
  • Low wing — the fuselage sits on top, interrupting airflow somewhat. Lift lands in between the other two, but the wing blocks the pilot's downward view.

Background — wing configuration

  • Biplane — two sets of wings. The extra wing area generates more lift, but it also generates more drag.
  • Canard — a small wing mounted forward of the main wing. It shifts the center of gravity farther rearward, which improves pitch control.

Vertical Stabilizer Designs

The vertical part of the empennage controls yaw — the aircraft's side-to-side pointing. Designers arrange it in different ways.

Background

  • Twin vertical stabilizers — two fins improve yaw control.
  • Triple vertical stabilizers — three fins improve yaw control, and can compensate for the limitations of other features of the design.
  • V-tail — replaces the conventional three tail surfaces (one vertical, two horizontal) with just two angled surfaces that each do part of both jobs. With fewer surfaces in the airflow, a V-tail is lighter and produces less drag.
